How to Calculate Your Macros for Weight Loss (Step by Step)

PlateAI TeamSeptember 24, 20267 min read

Most macro calculators hand you a split like 40/30/30 and call it a day. The trouble is that percentages hide what actually matters: how many grams of protein you're getting, and whether your fat is low enough to hurt. Here's a simple way to calculate macros for weight loss that starts from your bodyweight instead.

What are macros, and why track them?

Macros — short for macronutrients — are the three nutrients that supply your calories: protein, carbohydrate, and fat. Calories set whether you lose weight. Macros shape what you lose (fat versus muscle), how full you feel, and how well you train.

If you're new to tracking, start with our beginner's guide on how to count calories first — macros build directly on top of it.

Step 1: Set your calorie target

Everything else is carved out of this number. Estimate your maintenance calories (TDEE), then subtract about 300–500 kcal for steady weight loss. If you haven't done that yet, our guide to how many calories to eat to lose weight walks through it. For the examples below, we'll use a 160 lb (73 kg) person eating 1,800 kcal a day.

Step 2: Set protein first

Protein comes first because it's the macro with the most to gain from being set deliberately. In a calorie deficit, a higher intake helps preserve muscle and keeps hunger manageable. A practical range is 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight (about 0.7–1.0 g per lb) — see how much protein you need per day for the reasoning.

For our example we'll use 0.8 g per lb: 160 × 0.8 = 128 g of protein. Protein has 4 calories per gram, so that's 512 kcal.

Step 3: Set a fat floor

Fat is calorie-dense (9 kcal per gram), so it's easy to overshoot — but going too low backfires. Fat helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K and makes meals more satisfying. A sensible target for most people is roughly 0.3–0.45 g per lb of bodyweight, which usually lands between 20% and 35% of total calories.

For our example, 0.35 g per lb: 160 × 0.35 = 56 g of fat, or 504 kcal.

Step 4: Let carbs fill the rest

Carbohydrates are the flexible macro. Whatever calories remain after protein and fat go to carbs, at 4 kcal per gram:

1,800 kcal − 512 (protein) − 504 (fat) = 784 kcal ÷ 4 = about 196 g of carbs.

That gives a final target of roughly 128 g protein / 56 g fat / 196 g carbs — about 28% protein, 28% fat, and 44% carbs. Carbs fuel training, so if you lift or run regularly, this is the macro you want to protect.

Why grams beat percentages

A "40/30/30" split sounds precise, but percentages move with your calories. At 1,400 kcal, 30% protein is 105 g; at 2,800 kcal, it's 210 g — the same percentage, wildly different amounts. Setting protein and fat by bodyweight keeps the things your body actually needs stable, and lets carbs flex with your calorie target and activity.

Common macro mistakes

  • Chasing perfect ratios. Landing within about 10% of your protein target on most days matters far more than hitting an exact split every day.
  • Cutting fat too low. Going under the range above can leave you hungry and unsatisfied without speeding anything up.
  • Slashing carbs by default. Low-carb can work for some people, but cutting carbs too far often hurts training and adherence. Calories still decide weight loss.
  • Forgetting fiber. Aim for roughly 25–35 g a day from vegetables, fruit, legumes, and whole grains. It helps fullness and digestion.
  • Overlooking hidden fat. Oils, dressings, and cooking fats are easy to miss — see healthy foods that are surprisingly high in calories.

How to adjust your macros over time

Treat your first set of numbers as a draft. Track consistently for two to three weeks, then look at your weekly average weight, not a single morning's reading — our guide on how often to weigh yourself explains why. If the trend is flat despite consistent logging, trim about 100–150 calories, mostly from carbs or fat, and keep protein where it is. If energy or training tanks, add a little back.

Eating out or logging meals you didn't cook? A close estimate is fine — see how to track calories and macros when eating out.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best macro split for weight loss?

There isn't a single best split. What matters most is a calorie deficit you can sustain and enough protein. A common starting point is around 25–35% protein, 25–35% fat, and the rest carbs, but setting protein and fat by bodyweight, as above, is more reliable.

Do I need to hit my macros exactly?

No. Aim to be within roughly 10% of your protein and calorie targets on most days. Weekly averages matter more than any single day.

Are macros or calories more important for weight loss?

Calories determine whether you lose weight; macros influence how it goes — muscle retention, hunger, and performance. Get calories right first, then protein, then fine-tune the rest.

Should I track macros or just protein?

If tracking everything feels like too much, tracking calories and protein alone captures most of the benefit. Add carbs and fat once that feels automatic.

How many carbs should I eat to lose weight?

It depends on your calorie target and activity level. Carbs are what's left after protein and fat are set, which for many people falls between about 100 and 300 g a day.

The short version

  1. Set your calorie target — about 300–500 kcal below maintenance.
  2. Set protein at roughly 0.7–1.0 g per lb of bodyweight.
  3. Set fat at roughly 0.3–0.45 g per lb.
  4. Fill the remaining calories with carbs (4 kcal per gram).
  5. Review your weekly average after two to three weeks and adjust.
