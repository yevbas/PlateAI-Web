How to Count Calories: A Beginner's Step-by-Step Guide

PlateAI TeamSeptember 23, 20268 min read

Counting calories is one of the most reliable ways to lose weight, gain muscle, or just understand what you eat — and one of the easiest things to quit in the first week. Almost always that's because the method was too fiddly, not because you did anything wrong. Here's a beginner-friendly system that takes minutes a day.

What counting calories actually means

A calorie is a unit of energy. Food gives you calories; your body spends them on everything from breathing to running. If you eat about what you spend, your weight holds steady. Eat consistently less and it trends down; eat more and it trends up. Counting calories is just keeping a running tally of what goes in, so you can compare it with a target.

That's the whole idea. Everything below is about doing it with as little friction as possible — because the plan you actually keep doing beats the perfect one you drop after ten days.

Step 1: Set a daily calorie target

You need a number to count against. Start by estimating your maintenance calories (your Total Daily Energy Expenditure, or TDEE) with an online calculator or the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, then adjust for your goal:

  • Lose weight: eat roughly 300–500 calories below maintenance.
  • Maintain: eat at maintenance.
  • Build muscle: eat roughly 200–300 calories above maintenance.

Example: if your estimated maintenance is 2,200 kcal and you want to lose weight steadily, a starting target of about 1,800 kcal is reasonable. Treat it as a first draft, not a verdict — we cover how to refine it with real data in our guide on how many calories to eat to lose weight.

Step 2: Choose how you'll log your food

There are three common methods. None is "right" — they trade accuracy against effort, and effort is what decides whether you're still logging in month two.

Step 3: Log as you eat, not at night

The single biggest accuracy upgrade is timing. Logging a meal within a minute or two of eating takes seconds; reconstructing a whole day from memory at 10 p.m. means you'll forget the handful of nuts, the second coffee with milk, and the splash of oil in the pan. Those small misses add up to hundreds of calories a day.

Step 4: Know the numbers behind the numbers

Calories come from three macronutrients (plus alcohol), and each packs a different amount of energy per gram:

NutrientCalories per gram
Protein4
Carbohydrate4
Fat9
Alcohol7

That's why fat-heavy foods are so calorie-dense: a gram of fat carries more than twice the energy of a gram of protein or carbs. Once you're comfortable with calories, protein is the first macro worth tracking — it helps preserve muscle in a deficit and keeps you full. Where calories hide (and beginners get burned)

Most under-logging isn't the main dish — it's the extras. These are the usual suspects, with rough numbers:

Olive oil, 1 tbsp~120 kcal
Peanut butter, 2 tbsp~190 kcal
Latte, whole milk, 16 oz~190 kcal
Handful of almonds, 30 g~175 kcal
Ranch dressing, 2 tbsp~130 kcal
Glass of wine, 150 ml~125 kcal

The same goes for foods with a health halo — granola, smoothies, and avocado toast can quietly outweigh a normal meal. We break down why in healthy foods that are surprisingly high in calories.

Five beginner mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing up raw and cooked weights. 100 g of raw chicken breast is roughly 120 kcal; 100 g cooked is roughly 165, because water cooks off. Log using whichever state the entry is based on.
  • Skipping cooking oil, sauces, and drinks. They're easy to forget and calorie-dense.
  • Going all-or-nothing. One unlogged meal doesn't ruin a week. Log a rough estimate and move on rather than abandoning the day.
  • Setting the deficit too aggressive. Big cuts are hard to sustain. A modest deficit you can hold for months wins.
  • Judging progress by one day's weight. Daily weight is mostly water and noise. Read the trend over weeks — see how often you should weigh yourself.

What about restaurants and takeout?

You don't need a perfect number, just a defensible estimate. Log the closest match, add the oil or sauce you can see, and keep going. For a full system, read our guide to tracking calories when eating out.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate do I need to be?

Consistency matters more than precision. Being within 10% on most days is enough to see results, and errors tend to average out over a week. Exactness matters far less than logging regularly.

Can I count calories without a food scale?

Yes. A scale helps you learn portion sizes, but you can log with barcode scans, standard serving sizes, or photos. Many people use a scale for a couple of weeks to calibrate their eye, then rely on estimates.

How long should I keep counting calories?

Many people track for a few months until they have a good feel for portions and calorie-dense foods, then check in periodically. It's a skill you build, not a life sentence.

How many calories should a beginner eat?

It depends on your age, sex, height, weight, and activity. Most adults land somewhere between roughly 1,500 and 2,500 kcal per day; use a TDEE estimate for your starting point and refine it after two to three weeks of consistent logging.

Does calorie counting work for weight loss?

Weight change ultimately comes down to energy balance, and tracking intake is one of the most direct ways to manage it. People who log consistently generally do better than people who estimate from memory. It works best paired with adequate protein and a modest, sustainable deficit.

The short version

  1. Estimate your maintenance calories and set a target 300–500 kcal below it for weight loss.
  2. Pick the logging method you'll actually keep using.
  3. Log as you eat, including oils, sauces, and drinks.
  4. Watch protein once calories feel easy.
  5. Judge progress by the weekly trend, and adjust every two to three weeks.
